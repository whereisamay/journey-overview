01 · how VC works

The business behind the website

A short primer on early-stage venture, drawn from Sajith Pai's guide to breaking into venture. He's a VC at Blume, one of the funds compared in tab 02.

How a fund places its bets

A few winners carry the whole fund

A typical early-stage fund backs 25 to 35 companies. Blume usually puts $2–3M into each one. Because returns follow a power law, only about 3 to 5 of them need to do outstandingly well.

The 3–5 that return the fundSolid outcomesThe rest
Who they're betting on

Two markets for founders

First-time founders: the VC holds the power and sets the terms. A founder might raise a $2M seed with $250–500K in annual revenue (2023 figures).

Seasoned founders: the founder holds the power. They can raise $3–10M before any revenue. In this tier, “the asset picks the manager.”

Funds bet on experienced founders because their odds of success are seen as higher. So the best founders get to choose.

More sales than research

The product

A “brand of capital”

The VC sells the fund's money, the partner's time, the fund's brand signal and its platform services. The price is equity in the founder's company.

The job

Win the founder

Funds compete for the best founders, so the work looks like enterprise sales: persuading a founder to pick you. Early-stage deals aren't valued with spreadsheets of future cash flows, so persuasion matters more than finance skills.

The relationship

5 to 10 years together

After the cheque, the VC is a sparring partner, mentor and connector. Seniors also raise money from LPs, but the time spent on founders doesn't shrink.

Why this matters for the website. If venture is a sales job aimed at seasoned founders who get to choose, the website is the fund's sales collateral. It has to win a founder who has options. That's the lens for tab 02.

Source: Sajith Pai, Breaking into Venture: The Rough Guide. One investor's view, based mostly on Indian early-stage practice and 2023 figures.

02 · Stellaris, AJVC & Blume

Three funds, one page

What all three sites have in common, then how the businesses behind them differ.

What all three have in common

01

A go-getter hero line

Each opens with a big, motivational line aimed at an ambitious founder: “from ideas to infinity”, “We back the courageous to become entrepreneurs”, “May a thousand founders Blume”.

02

Famous portfolio names up front

Each puts its best-known investments near the top as proof. Stellaris tags every company with the stage it entered, AJVC groups logos by category, and Blume names status and outcomes.

03

Founders vouching for the fund

Each leans on founder testimonials: four quotes on Stellaris, dozens on AJVC (some even praise their rejection emails), and Unacademy's Gaurav Munjal for Blume.

What this means for Stellaris. It shares Blume's institutional model and its clean homepage, so it doesn't need AJVC's density or reach counters. What it leaves out is how it invests. A founder deciding whether to pitch wants to know what Stellaris looks for and how it makes a decision, and the homepage doesn't say.
